About

I am originally from Kolkata, India. Currently, I am a graduate student in Physics at The Ohio State University and a member of the Center for Cosmology and AstroParticle Physics (CCAPP).

My current research is centered on quantum field theory at finite temperature and density, with particular emphasis on perturbative descriptions of hot and dense matter. I am interested in the thermodynamics of relativistic gauge theories, scale separation and resummation, and in extending weak-coupling methods across physically relevant regimes of temperature and chemical potential.

My previous work has involved stochastic interacting systems, nonequilibrium statistical mechanics, and questions surrounding with random tensors.

You can reach out to me by email at: majumderswastik09@gmail.com

Research interests

I am broadly interested in analytical problems in statistical physics, particularly where field-theoretic, perturbative, many-body, and asymptotic methods intersect.

Hot and dense quark matter

Thermodynamics of relativistic matter at nonzero temperature and chemical potential, including problems motivated by dense QCD and the physics of strongly interacting matter.

Stochastic processes and nonequilibrium systems

Interacting stochastic systems, driven dynamics, transport, and collective behavior in low-dimensional and geometrically constrained systems.

Statistical mechanics

Analytical approaches to many-body statistical physics, emergent behavior, interacting particle systems, and systems with nontrivial geometry.

Random tensors

Eigenvalue and eigenvector distributions of higher-order random tensors and related questions at the interface of mathematical and statistical physics.

Personal essay

The Joy of the Climb

The recent deluge of scientific and mathematical progress driven by large language models is nothing short of a revolution. It may be the grandest answer yet to that ancient itch of our reptilian brain to automate mundane tasks. We physicists no longer find much joie de vivre in grinding through those nasty integrals. We have, we tell ourselves, a loftier task: contemplating what the result actually means. But an uncomfortable question lurks beneath that reassurance. What happens when these machines become better than us at the thinking, too? They already make a persuasive case. They have prodigious compute and a near-encyclopedic command of every corner of physics. With these, they can manipulate expressions and bring the relevant knowledge to bear on a problem in a few hours. The same problem might occupy our twenty-watt brains for weeks, or even months.

This sends me back to a more elementary question: why did I choose this path at all? Why do any of us devote our lives to one of the most rapidly flourishing sciences of the past three centuries? Is it to deepen our understanding of Nature, and, with luck, to publish in Nature along the way? I come from a family with barely any scientific background. I was drawn to physics in school simply because it seemed at once simple and deep. It reached into nearly everything happening around me, and understanding even a small piece of it brought a quiet, sublime joy. For a theorist, that joy is wonderfully inexpensive: a pinch of infinite patience and a few loose sheets of paper will do. There is also something in it that affirms the human will, our perennial and almost stubborn desire to truly understand. For me, the longing to know something deeply has always been the primary aim. That it also enlarges the frontier of human knowledge is a welcome companion on the journey.

Some who have made towering contributions to this enterprise believe that these formidable tools, with “some” human intervention, will let us make greater strides in physics than ever before. They may well be right. My disquiet is for people like me, who cherish the hike itself: the uncharted terrain, the unforeseen roadblocks, the occasional (non-fatal!) slip, and finally the hard won reverence of the summit. To be airlifted to the peak is not the same as having climbed it. I harbor no ambition to become a personal assistant to these machines, spending my days deciphering their calculations, proofs, and results. That is a rather dreary prospect for someone who has spent the better part of three lustrums believing that doing physics is a personal odyssey, an intimate pursuit of truths about our Universe.

Perhaps, then, the path forward is less a question of what these machines can do than of what we refuse to surrender. The summit will always be there for the taking. The climb, I suspect, will remain ours only if we insist on walking it.

November 12, 2026.
